Lecture 22

Adjugates and equivalent descriptions

What this lecture does

The determinant is unchanged by transposition, so it can be expanded along any row or column. These expansions give the adjugate and Cramer’s rule. Together with elimination, they describe the same invertible map through its equations, columns and determinant. A force-balance model supplies one application of the formulas.

Uses.

  • Summary 18: Independence means that a zero linear combination has only zero coefficients. A basis is independent and spans its space.

  • Summary 19: Transposition reverses products and transposition twice restores a matrix. The matrix’s columns specify its linear action.

  • Summary 20: Invertible square matrices are products of elementary matrices. Invertibility is equivalent to a zero null space and to unique solutions for every right side. The empty determinant is 1.

  • Summary 21: Row linearity and the row-operation rules are proved from the determinant definition. A determinant is nonzero exactly for invertible matrices, and determinants multiply.

22.1 Transposition and column rules

Let K=RK=\RK=R or K=CK=\CK=C, with all determinants taken for square matrices of size n≥1n\geq1n≥1 unless the empty convention is explicitly used.

Theorem 22.1 (Transpose invariance). For A∈Kn×nA\in K^{n\times n}A∈Kn×n, the determinant satisfies det⁡(AT)=det⁡A.\det(A^{\mathsf T})=\det A.det(AT)=detA.

Proof. First consider an elementary matrix EEE. A row-exchange elementary matrix equals its transpose, as does a row-scaling matrix. Their transposes therefore have the same determinants. A row-addition matrix has the entries of InI_nIn​ with an extra scalar ccc in position (i,j)(i,j)(i,j), where i≠ji\ne ji=j. Its transpose has that extra entry in position (j,i)(j,i)(j,i), so it is also a row-addition elementary matrix. Both determinants are 1 by the row-addition rule applied to InI_nIn​. Thus det⁡(ET)=det⁡E\det(E^{\mathsf T})=\det Edet(ET)=detE for every elementary type.

If AAA is invertible, Lecture 20 writes it as a finite product A=E1⋯EsA=E_1\cdots E_sA=E1​⋯Es​ of elementary matrices. Transposition reverses that order by Lecture 19, and multiplicativity gives det⁡(AT)=det⁡(EsT)⋯det⁡(E1T)=det⁡Es⋯det⁡E1=det⁡A.\det(A^{\mathsf T})=\det(E_s^{\mathsf T})\cdots\det(E_1^{\mathsf T}) =\det E_s\cdots\det E_1=\det A.det(AT)=det(EsT​)⋯det(E1T​)=detEs​⋯detE1​=detA. The last equality commutes scalar factors, not matrix factors. For the empty product A=InA=I_nA=In​, both determinants are 1.

If AAA is singular, its transpose is singular as well. Indeed, if ATA^{\mathsf T}AT had inverse CCC, transposing both identities ATC=CAT=InA^{\mathsf T}C=CA^{\mathsf T}=I_nATC=CAT=In​ would give CTA=ACT=InC^{\mathsf T}A=AC^{\mathsf T}=I_nCTA=ACT=In​ and make AAA invertible, a contradiction. Both determinants are therefore zero by Lecture 21’s criterion. This proves the equality also in the singular case. 

Rows of ATA^{\mathsf T}AT are columns of AAA. The theorem transfers every proved row rule to the corresponding column rule: the determinant is linear in each column separately, column swaps negate it, and adding a multiple of a different column preserves it. A zero column or equal columns gives determinant zero. For example, to scale one column, transpose, scale that row, apply the row rule and transpose back. Transpose invariance identifies the determinant before and after those two transpositions. The same argument gives each of the other column assertions with exactly its stated factor.

22.2 Expansion along any row or column

For A∈Kn×nA\in K^{n\times n}A∈Kn×n, let AijA_{ij}Aij​ denote the submatrix obtained by deleting row iii and column jjj. The corresponding cofactor is the scalar Cij=(−1)i+jdet⁡Aij.C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\det A_{ij}.Cij​=(−1)i+jdetAij​. At size 1 the remaining matrix is empty, whose determinant is 1. Thus the sole cofactor of a 1×11\times11×1 matrix is 1, even if its entry is zero.

Theorem 22.2 (Expansion formulas). For any fixed column j∈{1,…,n}j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}j∈{1,…,n} and fixed row i∈{1,…,n}i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}i∈{1,…,n}, det⁡A=∑k=1nakjCkj,det⁡A=∑k=1naikCik.\begin{equation} \label{eq:22:expansion} \det A=\sum_{k=1}^n a_{kj}C_{kj},\qquad \det A=\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}C_{ik}. \end{equation}detA=k=1∑n​akj​Ckj​,detA=k=1∑n​aik​Cik​.(22.1) Each expansion uses one complete column or one complete row.

Proof. Move column jjj to the first position by j−1j-1j−1 adjacent column swaps, keeping the other columns in their relative order. Call the matrix BBB. Its determinant is (−1)j−1det⁡A(-1)^{j-1}\det A(−1)j−1detA by the column-swap rule. Expanding BBB along its first column gives det⁡B=∑k=1n(−1)k+1akjdet⁡Akj.\det B=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k+1}a_{kj}\det A_{kj}.detB=k=1∑n​(−1)k+1akj​detAkj​. The minors are exactly AkjA_{kj}Akj​: deleting the moved column leaves every other column in its original relative order. Multiply the equation by (−1)j−1(-1)^{j-1}(−1)j−1. The sign in term kkk becomes (−1)k+j(-1)^{k+j}(−1)k+j, proving the column formula. This includes j=1j=1j=1 with no swaps.

Apply the column formula to ATA^{\mathsf T}AT. Its column iii is row iii of AAA, and its deleted submatrices are transposes of the corresponding deleted submatrices of AAA. Transpose invariance makes their determinants equal. The column formula for ATA^{\mathsf T}AT is therefore the stated row formula for AAA. At size 1 both formulas use the empty determinant 1 and give the single entry, so that boundary case is included. 

To compute an expansion, choose a row or column with few nonzero entries. Keep the cofactor sign separate from the sign of its matrix entry. The definition and the theorem give the same determinant; choosing a new expansion does not introduce a new convention.

22.3 The adjugate and its two products

The adjugate of AAA is the transpose of its cofactor matrix: adj⁡A=(Cij)T,(adj⁡A)ji=Cij.\begin{equation} \label{eq:22:adj-definition} \operatorname{adj}A=(C_{ij})^{\mathsf T},\qquad (\operatorname{adj}A)_{ji}=C_{ij}. \end{equation}adjA=(Cij​)T,(adjA)ji​=Cij​.(22.2) The adjugate is different from the adjoint A†A^\daggerA† of Lecture 19. The adjoint conjugates and transposes entries; the adjugate uses cofactors. Both have been defined also for singular square matrices.

For every A∈Kn×nA\in K^{n\times n}A∈Kn×n, Aadj⁡A=adj⁡A A=(det⁡A)In.\begin{equation} \label{eq:22:adj-products} A\operatorname{adj}A=\operatorname{adj}A\,A=(\det A)I_n. \end{equation}AadjA=adjAA=(detA)In​.(22.3) If det⁡A≠0\det A\ne0detA=0, its inverse is A−1=1det⁡Aadj⁡A.\begin{equation} \label{eq:22:adj-inverse} A^{-1}=\frac1{\det A}\operatorname{adj}A. \end{equation}A−1=detA1​adjA.(22.4)

If i≠ji\ne ji=j, replace row jjj of AAA by row iii. The new matrix has two equal rows and determinant zero. Its cofactors along row jjj are still CjkC_{jk}Cjk​, because deleting that row removes the only changed entries. Expanding along row jjj therefore gives ∑kaikCjk=0\sum_k a_{ik}C_{jk}=0∑k​aik​Cjk​=0. This proves the first product identity entry by entry.

The entry in row iii, column jjj of adj⁡A A\operatorname{adj}A\,AadjAA is ∑kCkiakj\sum_k C_{ki}a_{kj}∑k​Cki​akj​. If i=ji=ji=j, expansion along column iii gives det⁡A\det AdetA. If i≠ji\ne ji=j, replace column iii by column jjj. The new matrix has equal columns and determinant zero. Its cofactors along column iii remain CkiC_{ki}Cki​, since their deletion removes the changed column. Expanding along that column gives ∑kCkiakj=0\sum_k C_{ki}a_{kj}=0∑k​Cki​akj​=0. 22.4 Cramer’s rule and its hypothesis

For b∈Knb\in K^nb∈Kn, write A[j←b]A[j\leftarrow b]A[j←b] for the matrix obtained by replacing column jjj of AAA by bbb, leaving every other column unchanged.

Theorem 22.4 (Cramer’s rule). If A∈Kn×nA\in K^{n\times n}A∈Kn×n and det⁡A≠0\det A\ne0detA=0, the unique solution of Ax=bAx=bAx=b is xj=det⁡A[j←b]det⁡A,j=1,…,n.x_j=\frac{\det A[j\leftarrow b]}{\det A},\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.xj​=detAdetA[j←b]​,j=1,…,n.

Proof. The nonzero determinant guarantees a unique solution by Lectures 20–21. Write the columns of AAA as a1,…,ana_1,\ldots,a_na1​,…,an​. For that solution, the column-action formula gives b=∑kxkakb=\sum_k x_ka_kb=∑k​xk​ak​. Expand the determinant of A[j←b]A[j\leftarrow b]A[j←b] by linearity in column jjj: det⁡A[j←b]=∑k=1nxkdet⁡A[j←ak].\det A[j\leftarrow b]=\sum_{k=1}^n x_k\det A[j\leftarrow a_k].detA[j←b]=k=1∑n​xk​detA[j←ak​]. If k≠jk\ne jk=j, that matrix has two equal columns and determinant zero. If k=jk=jk=j, it is AAA itself. Thus the sum is xjdet⁡Ax_j\det Axj​detA. Division by the assumed nonzero denominator gives the formula for every jjj. 

For singular systems, elimination is still required. Even zero values of every replacement determinant do not by themselves guarantee consistency. For example, take D=(100000000),b=(010).D=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad b=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}.D=​100​000​000​​,b=​010​​. The determinant of DDD and all three replacement determinants are zero: each replacement matrix retains at least one zero column. Nevertheless, the second equation of Dx=bDx=bDx=b is 0=10=10=1, a contradiction. Cramer’s quotients do not apply at a zero denominator.

The column connections follow as well, without a general basis-cardinality theorem. A zero column combination is exactly Ax=0nAx=0_nAx=0n​, so column independence is equivalent to a zero null space. If AAA is invertible, x=A−1bx=A^{-1}bx=A−1b expresses every target bbb as a column combination. Thus its columns span KnK^nKn.

Conversely, suppose the columns span KnK^nKn. Reduce AAA to R=EAR=EAR=EA by an invertible product of elementary matrices. If RRR had a zero row in position qqq, choose b=E−1eqb=E^{-1}e_qb=E−1eq​. Spanning would give a solution of Ax=bAx=bAx=b, and hence of Rx=Eb=eqRx=Eb=e_qRx=Eb=eq​. Its row qqq would read 0=10=10=1, a contradiction. Thus RRR has no zero row, has nnn pivots and is InI_nIn​. The inversion criterion proves that AAA is invertible. Finally, a basis means independence and spanning by Lecture 18, so the basis description is equivalent to the same conditions. Summary

  • Theorem 22.1: transposition preserves the determinant. The row rules therefore hold for columns as well.
  • Theorem 22.2: one complete row or column, with its cofactors Cij=(−1)i+jdet⁡AijC_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\det A_{ij}Cij​=(−1)i+jdetAij​, gives the determinant.
  • Theorem 22.3: the adjugate transposes the cofactor matrix and satisfies both product identities for every square AAA, including singular AAA. For det⁡A≠0\det A\ne0detA=0, A−1=adj⁡A/det⁡AA^{-1}=\operatorname{adj}A/\det AA−1=adjA/detA.
  • Theorem 22.4: nonzero determinant gives each solution coordinate as a replacement-column determinant divided by det⁡A\det AdetA. At a zero denominator, use elimination.
  • §22.5: invertibility, the square elimination and system criteria, a basis of columns, and nonzero determinant describe the same condition. In force models, algebraic solutions must also satisfy the stated physical restrictions.
