Lecture 17

Linear systems and elimination

Elimination replaces equations by equivalent ones until the unknowns can be read in terms of free parameters. We prove why the row operations preserve every solution and why the procedure stops. A single parameter family then gives a unique solution, a family of solutions or a contradiction. The independence of the pivot count from the reduction route is a separate cited result.

Uses.

  • Summary 1: Equivalent statements have the same truth value; a system requires all its equations simultaneously.

  • Summary 2: Real arithmetic permits division by a nonzero scalar.

  • Summary 12: Ordered lists record components, with equality component by component.

  • Summary 15: Complex arithmetic has the same associative and distributive laws, with a reciprocal for every nonzero number.

17.2 Elementary row operations preserve solutions

Two systems in the same unknowns are equivalent if their solution sets are equal. Each row of the augmented matrix represents one whole equation, including its right side. Denote its current row by RiR_iRi​. The elementary row operations are:

  1. exchange two rows, Ri↔RjR_i\leftrightarrow R_jRi​↔Rj​;

  2. multiply one row by a nonzero scalar c∈Kc\in Kc∈K;

  3. replace RiR_iRi​ by Ri+cRjR_i+cR_jRi​+cRj​, where i≠ji\ne ji=j and c∈Kc\in Kc∈K.

Every entry in the chosen row is changed, including its right side. The last operation leaves the other row RjR_jRj​ unchanged.

Theorem 17.1 (Solution preservation). Each elementary row operation produces an equivalent system. Every finite sequence of these operations therefore preserves the entire solution set.

Proof. Exchanging equations changes only their order, not the requirement that all hold. Multiplying an equality by ccc preserves it, and division by the nonzero ccc recovers the original equality. Thus the first two operations work in both directions.

For row addition, every solution of the old system satisfies the sum of its row-iii equation and ccc times its row-jjj equation. It therefore satisfies the new row-iii equation and all unchanged equations. Conversely, the new system still includes the unchanged row-jjj equation. Subtracting ccc times it from the new row-iii equation recovers the old one. This proves equality of solution sets. Applying this equality successively proves the finite-sequence statement. 

Scaling a row by zero is excluded because it cannot be reversed. For instance, it would replace x1=1x_1=1x1​=1 by 0=00=00=0 and lose a restriction. A zero candidate cannot be used as a divisor. A row exchange may supply a nonzero entry instead.

17.3 Echelon form and the finite elimination procedure

A zero row has all entries zero, including the augmented entry when there is one. The leading entry of a nonzero row is its first nonzero entry from the left. A matrix is in row-echelon form if its zero rows are at the bottom and the leading entry of each successive nonzero row is strictly to the right of the leading entry in the row above. A pivot in an echelon matrix is such a leading entry. The echelon conditions imply zero entries below every pivot. A matrix is in reduced row-echelon form if it is in echelon form, every pivot is 1 and every other entry in a pivot column is zero.

Proposition 17.2 (Elimination). Every finite matrix over KKK can be put into row-echelon form and then reduced row-echelon form by elementary row operations.

Proof. Start with the first row and column. Among the remaining rows, find the leftmost column with any nonzero entry. If no such column exists, all remaining rows are zero and the process stops. Otherwise exchange rows to put one of its nonzero entries in the next available row. This entry is the pivot. For each row below, subtract the pivot row multiplied by the quotient of that row’s pivot-column entry by the nonzero pivot. The pivot-column entries below it become zero.

Repeat on the rows below and columns to the right. The unprocessed rows have zeros in the earlier pivot columns and in the columns that were skipped as zero. Subtracting the pivot row preserves these zeros because its entries to the left of its pivot are zero. Each step uses a new row and a strictly later column. There are finitely many rows and columns, so the process stops and has the required echelon conditions. This is Gaussian elimination.

To reduce further, start with the bottom pivot row and divide it by its nonzero pivot. Subtract multiples of it from all rows above to clear the pivot column. Continue upwards. A pivot row has zeros to the left of its pivot, so earlier leading positions are unchanged. Its entries in the later pivot columns have already been cleared, so the operation does not undo those cleared columns. After finitely many rows, every pivot is 1 and is the sole nonzero entry in its column. This is Gauss–Jordan elimination. 

The proposition proves that a reduction can be performed. It does not yet claim that two routes have the same reduced form. For solving a given system, solution preservation holds along the route actually used.

A contradiction

A nonzero augmented entry after zero coefficient entries is a contradiction, whereas an entirely zero row is an identity. These two row types have different effects even though both have zero coefficients of the unknowns.

17.5 The consistency criterion and all free parameters

In any echelon form of an augmented matrix, call a column among the first nnn a pivot column if it contains a pivot. Once contradictions have been excluded, each nonpivot unknown is called free. Give different free variables separate parameters. Let rrr be the number of coefficient pivot columns in this particular reduction. There are n−rn-rn−r nonpivot columns.

Theorem 17.3 (Consistency and free-variable criterion). For an echelon form obtained from [A∣b][A\mid b][A∣b] by row operations, the system is consistent exactly when the augmented column has no pivot. Equivalently, the coefficient block and the full augmented matrix in that form have equally many pivots. If consistent, its solutions are parameterised by arbitrary values of the n−rn-rn−r free variables. It has one solution when r=nr=nr=n and infinitely many when r<nr<nr<n.

Proof. A pivot in column n+1n+1n+1 is the first nonzero entry of its row. All preceding coefficient entries are zero, so that row is 0=c0=c0=c with c≠0c\ne0c=0. It prevents any solution. Conversely, if the augmented column has no pivot, every row with zero coefficients also has zero right side. Reduce the echelon matrix further by Gauss–Jordan operations. These operations preserve the pivot positions as shown in the elimination proof, so there is still no augmented pivot.

Choose arbitrary values for each free unknown. In each nonzero reduced row, its pivot is 1 and the entries in all other pivot columns are zero. That row determines its one pivot unknown by subtracting the free-variable terms from its right side. Every row is now satisfied, and zero rows read 0=00=00=0. This constructs a solution for every free assignment. Every solution must satisfy the same pivot equations, so no further solutions exist and the assignment determines the pivot entries uniquely.

If there are no free variables, the construction gives exactly one solution. If there is a free variable, varying it through KKK changes that component and hence gives infinitely many distinct solutions. Finally, removing the augmented column removes one pivot precisely when that column contains a pivot. This proves the equivalent pivot-count criterion in the actual form under consideration. 

The theorem includes a matrix with no coefficient pivots. If its right sides also vanish, every unknown is free and every column x∈Knx\in K^nx∈Kn is a solution. A nonzero right side instead produces a contradiction. The number of equations alone does not settle consistency. 17.6 Uniqueness of reduced form

Theorem 17.4 (Uniqueness of reduced form cited). A matrix over R\RR or C\CC has a unique reduced row-echelon form under row operations. In particular, its pivot columns and pivot count are independent of the elimination route.

The uniqueness result is used without proof; see Lay, Lay and McDonald, §1.2, Theorem 1. The connection to pivot counts is that normalising pivots and clearing above them does not move their columns. Every echelon form therefore has the same pivot columns as its reduced form. Uniqueness makes those columns, and hence their number, independent of the starting choices during elimination. This route-independence statement retains the cited status.

The earlier consistency proof applies to any actual reduction and did not use uniqueness of reduced form. Lecture 18 will use the route-independent coefficient pivot count to define rank and a dimension convention for the homogeneous solution set. Its dependence on this cited result will be explicit.

Summary

  • Equation (17.1): a linear system is encoded by its coefficient matrix and right-side column. The augmented column represents constants, not a further unknown.
  • Theorem 17.1: the three reversible row operations preserve the whole solution set. Scaling and pivot division require nonzero scalars.
  • Proposition 17.2: Gaussian and Gauss–Jordan elimination terminate in echelon and reduced echelon forms.
  • Theorem 17.3: an augmented pivot is exactly a contradiction. Otherwise each free assignment determines exactly one solution, giving one solution with no free variable and infinitely many when one is present.
  • Theorem 17.4 cited: uniqueness of reduced form and route-independent pivot counts are separate cited facts.
