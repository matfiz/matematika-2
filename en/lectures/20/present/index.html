Lecture 20

Inversion and determinant definitions

Elimination decides whether a square matrix has an inverse and, when it does, constructs that inverse. The same calculation solves a system for every right side. We then define the determinant by first-column expansion and identify its real geometric meaning in dimensions two and three. The relation between determinants and invertibility is proved in Lecture 21.

Uses.

  • Summary 13: The cross-product length gives parallelogram area, and its oriented normal fixes the turn direction. The scalar triple product is signed volume; its absolute value is the parallelepiped’s volume.

  • Summary 17: Row operations preserve solutions, and elimination terminates. A free-variable assignment determines a unique solution of a consistent reduced system.

  • Summary 18: The null space contains all homogeneous solutions. Its special free-variable columns give a basis. Rank is the cited intrinsic pivot count.

  • Summary 19: Compatible products represent composition. Multiplication is associative. Elementary matrices are invertible and act by row operations. An inverse is unique and satisfies both product identities.

20.1 Inverse products and the elimination criteria

Let K=RK=\RK=R or K=CK=\CK=C and let A∈Kn×nA\in K^{n\times n}A∈Kn×n, where n≥1n\geq1n≥1. By Lecture 19, an inverse satisfies AA−1=A−1A=InAA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I_nAA−1=A−1A=In​. Both identities are part of the definition. A single identity will not be used as a substitute for the definition in this lecture. Sheet 20 proves the additional square-matrix result that one identity implies the other.

First, if UUU and VVV are invertible matrices of the same square size, then their product is invertible and its inverse is (UV)−1=V−1U−1.\begin{equation} \label{eq:20:product-inverse} (UV)^{-1}=V^{-1}U^{-1}. \end{equation}(UV)−1=V−1U−1.(20.1) To prove this, use associativity from Lecture 19 in both orders: (UV)(V−1U−1)=U(VV−1)U−1=In,(V−1U−1)(UV)=V−1(U−1U)V=In.(UV)(V^{-1}U^{-1})=U(VV^{-1})U^{-1}=I_n,\qquad (V^{-1}U^{-1})(UV)=V^{-1}(U^{-1}U)V=I_n.(UV)(V−1U−1)=U(VV−1)U−1=In​,(V−1U−1)(UV)=V−1(U−1U)V=In​. Thus the proposed matrix satisfies the two defining identities. Repeated application shows that a finite product of invertible matrices is invertible. Its inverse has the reversed order of the individual inverses. The product of no factors is defined to be InI_nIn​ and has inverse InI_nIn​.

Choose any sequence of elementary row operations that reduces AAA to a reduced row-echelon matrix RRR. If their elementary matrices are E1,…,EsE_1,\ldots,E_sE1​,…,Es​, put E=Es⋯E1,R=EA.E=E_s\cdots E_1,\qquad R=EA.E=Es​⋯E1​,R=EA. Every factor is invertible by the reverse-operation argument of Lecture 19. Therefore EEE is invertible by Equation (20.1). For a sequence with no operations, take E=InE=I_nE=In​.

Theorem 20.1 (Square-matrix inversion criteria). For A∈Kn×nA\in K^{n\times n}A∈Kn×n, the following conditions are equivalent:

  1. The matrix AAA is invertible.

  2. The homogeneous system Ax=0nAx=0_nAx=0n​ has only the solution x=0nx=0_nx=0n​.

  3. A reduction of AAA has nnn coefficient pivots.

  4. The reduced row-echelon matrix obtained from AAA is InI_nIn​.

When these conditions hold, AAA is a product of elementary matrices.

Proof. If AAA is invertible and Ax=0nAx=0_nAx=0n​, multiply by A−1A^{-1}A−1 to get x=A−1Ax=A−10n=0nx=A^{-1}Ax=A^{-1}0_n=0_nx=A−1Ax=A−10n​=0n​. Thus invertibility implies the homogeneous condition.

Suppose that homogeneous condition holds. If a chosen reduction had a free column, assign 1 in that free coordinate and 0 in the other free coordinates. The consistency theorem of Lecture 17 would give a nonzero homogeneous solution, a contradiction. Hence all nnn columns have pivots. Conversely, with nnn coefficient pivots there are no free columns, and the homogeneous equations force the zero solution by the same theorem.

In an n×nn\times nn×n matrix, nnn pivots occupy every row and column. In reduced form each pivot is 1 and its column has no other nonzero entry. Their left-to-right order therefore gives exactly R=InR=I_nR=In​. The identity matrix also has nnn pivots, proving the converse connection.

Finally, if EA=InEA=I_nEA=In​ for the invertible product EEE defined above, left multiplication by E−1E^{-1}E−1 gives A=E−1A=E^{-1}A=E−1. The two identities AE=EA=InAE=EA=I_nAE=EA=In​ now follow, so AAA is invertible with inverse EEE. Moreover, A=E1−1⋯Es−1A=E_1^{-1}\cdots E_s^{-1}A=E1−1​⋯Es−1​ is a product of elementary matrices because each inverse is the elementary matrix of the reverse operation. For A=InA=I_nA=In​ the empty product gives the stated conclusion. These implications prove all equivalences. 

This proof uses an actual reduction. It does not use a determinant. Writing the pivot condition as rank⁡A=n\rank A=nrankA=n uses the rank convention of Lecture 18, whose route-independent meaning inherits the cited input of Lecture 17. The proof above gives the same answer for every actual reduction.

An invertible matrix also makes Ax=bAx=bAx=b uniquely solvable for every b∈Knb\in K^nb∈Kn. The solution is x=A−1b.x=A^{-1}b.x=A−1b. It satisfies Ax=AA−1b=bAx=AA^{-1}b=bAx=AA−1b=b. Every solution must have that value after multiplying its equation by A−1A^{-1}A−1. Conversely, unique solvability for every right side includes the homogeneous case b=0nb=0_nb=0n​, so Theorem 20.1 proves invertibility. Thus invertibility decides all right sides at once.

20.2 Gauss–Jordan inversion

To construct the inverse, attach InI_nIn​ to the square coefficient matrix: [A∣In].[A\mid I_n].[A∣In​]. Reduce the left block by elementary row operations, applying every operation to the entire augmented row. After a sequence with product EEE, the blocks are [EA∣E][EA\mid E][EA∣E], since the initial right block is InI_nIn​. If the left block reaches InI_nIn​, Theorem 20.1 proves E=A−1E=A^{-1}E=A−1. The final array is therefore [In∣A−1][I_n\mid A^{-1}][In​∣A−1]. If the reduced left block is not InI_nIn​, the same theorem proves that no inverse exists. The procedure stops with that decision; pivots in the right block do not supply missing coefficient pivots.

20.3 The recursive determinant

For a square matrix A∈Kn×nA\in K^{n\times n}A∈Kn×n, its determinant is a scalar written det⁡A\det AdetA. If n=1n=1n=1, define det⁡(a11)=a11\det(a_{11})=a_{11}det(a11​)=a11​. For n≥2n\geq2n≥2, let Ai1A_{i1}Ai1​ be the (n−1)×(n−1)(n-1)\times(n-1)(n−1)×(n−1) matrix obtained by deleting row iii and column 1. Define the determinant recursively by expansion along the first column: det⁡A=∑i=1n(−1)i+1ai1det⁡Ai1.\begin{equation} \label{eq:20:det-definition} \det A=\sum_{i=1}^n(-1)^{i+1}a_{i1}\det A_{i1}. \end{equation}detA=i=1∑n​(−1)i+1ai1​detAi1​.(20.2) The smaller determinants have already been defined by the recursion. The procedure ends at size 1, so it defines exactly one scalar for every positive square size. For later formulas involving a deleted row and column of a 1×11\times11×1 matrix, we also set the determinant of the empty 0×00\times00×0 matrix to 1. With that convention the same expansion works at size 1 as well. No determinant is assigned here to a nonsquare matrix.

At size 2 the definition gives det⁡(abcd)=ad−cb.\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=ad-cb.det(ac​bd​)=ad−cb. At size 3, applying the same definition once and then the size-2 formula gives det⁡(abcdefghk)=a(ek−fh)−d(bk−ch)+g(bf−ce).\begin{align*} \det\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\d&e&f\\g&h&k\end{pmatrix} &=a(ek-fh)-d(bk-ch)+g(bf-ce). \end{align*}det​adg​beh​cfk​​​=a(ek−fh)−d(bk−ch)+g(bf−ce).​ The signs along the first column are +,−,++,-,++,−,+. Every smaller determinant retains the original order of its remaining rows and columns. No row swap is implicit in deleting a row and column.

For the earlier invertible example, the definition gives det⁡A=1(1⋅1−1⋅0)−0(2⋅1−0⋅0)+2(2⋅1−0⋅1)=5.\det A=1(1\cdot1-1\cdot0)-0(2\cdot1-0\cdot0) +2(2\cdot1-0\cdot1)=5.detA=1(1⋅1−1⋅0)−0(2⋅1−0⋅0)+2(2⋅1−0⋅1)=5. The number 5 also occurred as the final coefficient pivot in this particular elimination. We have not used that observation to prove a general determinant rule. Lecture 21 proves the row-operation rules and the invertibility criterion from this definition.

A matrix is upper triangular if all entries strictly below the diagonal are zero. For such a matrix the first-column expansion has only its first term, and its remaining minor is upper triangular again. Induction, starting from size 1, therefore gives det⁡A=a11a22⋯annfor upper triangular A.\det A=a_{11}a_{22}\cdots a_{nn}\quad\text{for upper triangular }A.detA=a11​a22​⋯ann​for upper triangular A. In particular det⁡In=1\det I_n=1detIn​=1, including the stated empty convention. This calculation is a direct use of the definition, without row operations.

20.4 Signed area and volume

For real planar columns u=(u1,u2)u=(u_1,u_2)u=(u1​,u2​) and v=(v1,v2)v=(v_1,v_2)v=(v1​,v2​), extend them to u~=(u1,u2,0)\widetilde u=(u_1,u_2,0)u=(u1​,u2​,0) and v~=(v1,v2,0)\widetilde v=(v_1,v_2,0)v=(v1​,v2​,0) in R3\R^3R3. With the positive normal in the third coordinate direction, define the signed area to be the third component u1v2−u2v1u_1v_2-u_2v_1u1​v2​−u2​v1​. The size-2 determinant formula therefore gives signed area det⁡[ u v ]\det[\,u\ v\,]det[u v] and ordinary area ∣det⁡[ u v ]∣|\det[\,u\ v\,]|∣det[u v]∣. Zero or parallel columns give zero area.

For real columns u,v,w∈R3u,v,w\in\R^3u,v,w∈R3, Lecture 13 gives signed volume u⋅(v×w)u\cdot(v\times w)u⋅(v×w). Expanding the determinant with these columns gives det⁡[ u v w ]=u1(v2w3−v3w2)−u2(v1w3−v3w1)+u3(v1w2−v2w1)=u⋅(v×w).\begin{align*} \det[\,u\ v\ w\,] &=u_1(v_2w_3-v_3w_2)-u_2(v_1w_3-v_3w_1) +u_3(v_1w_2-v_2w_1)\\ &=u\cdot(v\times w). \end{align*}det[u v w]​=u1​(v2​w3​−v3​w2​)−u2​(v1​w3​−v3​w1​)+u3​(v1​w2​−v2​w1​)=u⋅(v×w).​ The last equality is the previously derived component formula for the scalar triple product. Thus the determinant is signed volume, and its absolute value is the ordinary volume, including zero for coplanar columns.

In the running example, the three columns are u=(1,0,2)u=(1,0,2)u=(1,0,2), v=(2,1,0)v=(2,1,0)v=(2,1,0) and w=(0,1,1)w=(0,1,1)w=(0,1,1). Their cross product and signed volume are v×w=(1,−2,2),u⋅(v×w)=1+0+4=5.v\times w=(1,-2,2),\qquad u\cdot(v\times w)=1+0+4=5.v×w=(1,−2,2),u⋅(v×w)=1+0+4=5. The parallelepiped therefore has volume 5 in the corresponding coordinate volume units. This agrees with the computed determinant. The area and volume interpretations concern real columns. The recursive determinant itself remains defined over C\CC by the same finite arithmetic, without assigning a planar-area meaning to its complex value.

Summary

  • Equation (20.1): invertible products have inverse factors in reverse order. Both defining inverse identities are verified.
  • Theorem 20.1: for square AAA, invertibility, a zero null space, nnn pivots and reduced form InI_nIn​ are equivalent. Such a matrix is a product of elementary matrices and solves every right side uniquely.
  • Reducing the left block of [A∣In][A\mid I_n][A∣In​] to InI_nIn​ produces [In∣A−1][I_n\mid A^{-1}][In​∣A−1]. A missing coefficient pivot means that no inverse exists.
  • Equation (20.2): the determinant is defined by first-column expansion, with the stated size-1 and empty conventions. Upper triangular matrices have the product of their diagonal entries as determinant.
  • For real columns, det⁡[ u v ]\det[\,u\ v\,]det[u v] is signed area and det⁡[ u v w ]=u⋅(v×w)\det[\,u\ v\ w\,]=u\cdot(v\times w)det[u v w]=u⋅(v×w) is signed volume. Their absolute values give ordinary area and volume.

Sheet 20 practices inversion and determinants and proves the promised one-sided inverse result. Lecture 21 proves that a nonzero determinant is equivalent to invertibility.
