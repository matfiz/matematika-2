Lecture 26

Continuous integrands and the fundamental theorem

What this lecture does

On a closed finite interval, uniform continuity makes continuous functions integrable. We then study an integral whose upper endpoint moves. Its derivative recovers the integrand at interior continuity points. This gives the endpoint formula for a definite integral and proves the definite substitution and parts rules.

26.1 Continuous functions are integrable

Theorem 26.1 (Continuous integrability). Every continuous real function on [a,b][a,b][a,b] is Riemann integrable.

Proof. The extreme value theorem, Theorem 7.1, bounds fff on [a,b][a,b][a,b]. In each closed subinterval, every pair x,yx,yx,y satisfies f(x)≤f(y)+ηf(x)\leq f(y)+\etaf(x)≤f(y)+η. Consequently, U(P,f)−L(P,f)≤∑iηΔxi=η(b−a)=ε2<ε.U(P,f)-L(P,f)\leq\sum_i\eta\Delta x_i =\eta(b-a)=\frac\varepsilon2<\varepsilon.U(P,f)−L(P,f)≤i∑​ηΔxi​=η(b−a)=2ε​<ε. Uniform continuity supplies one mesh size for the whole interval. Continuity at separate points alone would give different local sizes. Heine–Cantor is the proved step that permits their replacement by one choice. No differentiation theorem was needed for this conclusion.

26.2 Finitely many discontinuities

A discontinuity is a domain point at which a function is not continuous. The following result is recorded with a proof sketch [S].

Theorem 26.2 (Finite-discontinuity integrability; sketch status). If f:[a,b]→Rf:[a,b]\to\Rf:[a,b]→R is bounded and has only finitely many discontinuities, then fff is Riemann integrable.

Proof sketch. Choose M>0M>0M>0 with ∣f∣≤M|f|\leq M∣f∣≤M. Fix ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0. Cover the finitely many discontinuities by open intervals, truncated at aaa and bbb, whose total length is below ε/(4M)\varepsilon/(4M)ε/(4M). Choose their interior boundary points outside the finite set of discontinuities. Endpoint neighbourhoods may be one-sided. Merge overlapping intervals.

Ignore empty or zero-length complementary pieces. If there are none, only the covered-cell estimate below is needed. Each remaining closed piece contains no discontinuities. The restriction of fff to each piece is continuous and hence uniformly continuous by Heine–Cantor. There are only finitely many pieces, so one positive mesh choice bounds their subinterval oscillations by η=ε/(2(b−a))\eta=\varepsilon/(2(b-a))η=ε/(2(b−a)). Refine separately on these pieces and include all covering-interval boundary points in the partition. The covered cells contribute less than 2M⋅ε/(4M)=ε/22M\cdot\varepsilon/(4M)=\varepsilon/22M⋅ε/(4M)=ε/2 to the Darboux gap. The other cells contribute at most η(b−a)=ε/2\eta(b-a)=\varepsilon/2η(b−a)=ε/2. The total gap is less than ε\varepsilonε, proving integrability by the criterion. If there are no discontinuities, use the continuous case.

26.6 Definite substitution and integration by parts

For these rules, all differentiable functions mentioned are defined on open intervals containing the relevant closed intervals. Their derivatives are required to be continuous on the closed intervals where they are used. This states the endpoint regularity needed by the integral formulas.

  • Theorems 26.1 and 26.2: continuous functions on [a,b][a,b][a,b] are integrable [P]; bounded functions with finitely many discontinuities are integrable [S]. Here a<ba<ba<b.

  • Theorem 26.4: for an integrable fff, its accumulation integral is continuous and has derivative f(x)f(x)f(x) at every interior continuity point.
  • Theorems 26.7 to 26.9: continuity and an antiderivative give endpoint evaluation. The definite substitution and parts rules follow from the chain and product rules with the stated continuity hypotheses.
