Lecture 19

Matrices and composition

A matrix describes how a linear map acts on coordinate columns. Applying two such maps in succession determines their matrix product. This interpretation proves the product laws, fixes the order of every operation and expresses row operations by multiplication.

Uses.

  • Summary 2: A composition applies the rightmost function first. Equality of functions means equality on every input.

  • Summary 12: Columns have componentwise addition and scalar multiplication. The standard coordinate columns are independent and span the coordinate space.

  • Summary 15: Complex conjugation preserves sums and products. The local 2×22\times 22×2 product represents multiplication of complex numbers.

  • Summary 17: A matrix has a specified number of rows and columns. Its action on a column records a linear system. Elementary row operations preserve the solutions of a system.

  • Summary 18: The coefficient action is linear. A basis gives unique coordinates, and the null space consists of the columns sent to zero.

19.1 Matrix operations and columns

Keep the scalar field K=RK=\RK=R or K=CK=\CK=C fixed. For positive integers mmm and nnn, write Km×nK^{m\times n}Km×n for the matrices with mmm rows and nnn columns and entries in KKK. Two matrices are equal if their sizes agree and their corresponding entries agree. For A,B∈Km×nA,B\in K^{m\times n}A,B∈Km×n and c∈Kc\in Kc∈K, define addition and scalar multiplication by (A+B)ij=aij+bij,(cA)ij=caij,1≤i≤m,1≤j≤n.(A+B)_{ij}=a_{ij}+b_{ij},\qquad (cA)_{ij}=ca_{ij}, \qquad 1\leq i\leq m,\quad 1\leq j\leq n.(A+B)ij​=aij​+bij​,(cA)ij​=caij​,1≤i≤m,1≤j≤n. The zero matrix 0m×n0_{m\times n}0m×n​ has every entry zero. These entrywise operations inherit the associative and distributive scalar laws. Addition is commutative and −A-A−A is the additive inverse of AAA, since each corresponding scalar assertion holds in every entry. Addition of matrices of different sizes is undefined.

Denote the columns of AAA by a1,…,an∈Kma_1,\ldots,a_n\in K^ma1​,…,an​∈Km. The coefficient action from Lecture 17 has the column expression Ax=x1a1+⋯+xnan,x∈Kn.\begin{equation} \label{eq:19:column-action} Ax=x_1a_1+\cdots+x_na_n,\qquad x\in K^n. \end{equation}Ax=x1​a1​+⋯+xn​an​,x∈Kn.(19.1) Indeed, its iiith entry is ∑jaijxj\sum_j a_{ij}x_j∑j​aij​xj​, the previously defined action. For the standard coordinate column ej∈Kne_j\in K^nej​∈Kn, only coordinate jjj is 1. Consequently Aej=ajAe_j=a_jAej​=aj​: a matrix’s action on eje_jej​ recovers its jjjth column. The standard columns in different coordinate spaces have their sizes supplied by the expression in which they occur.

A map T:Kn→KmT:K^n\to K^mT:Kn→Km is linear over KKK if T(cu+dv)=cT(u)+dT(v)(u,v∈Kn, c,d∈K).T(cu+dv)=cT(u)+dT(v)\qquad(u,v\in K^n,\ c,d\in K).T(cu+dv)=cT(u)+dT(v)(u,v∈Kn, c,d∈K). The coefficient action x↦Axx\mapsto Axx↦Ax is linear by Equation (18.2). Conversely, every linear map has exactly one matrix in the standard coordinates. To prove existence, use x=∑jxjejx=\sum_j x_je_jx=∑j​xj​ej​ and linearity repeatedly to obtain T(x)=∑j=1nxjT(ej).T(x)=\sum_{j=1}^n x_jT(e_j).T(x)=j=1∑n​xj​T(ej​). Thus the matrix with columns T(e1),…,T(en)T(e_1),\ldots,T(e_n)T(e1​),…,T(en​) has action TTT by Equation (19.1). For uniqueness, any matrix with that action must have these columns, since evaluating its action at each eje_jej​ recovers them. In particular, matrices of the same size with equal actions on every column are equal.

19.2 Multiplication from successive actions

Let A∈Km×nA\in K^{m\times n}A∈Km×n and B∈Kn×pB\in K^{n\times p}B∈Kn×p. The map x↦A(Bx)x\mapsto A(Bx)x↦A(Bx) sends KpK^pKp to KmK^mKm and is linear: linearity of BBB and then of AAA takes a linear combination to the same combination of the two output columns. Define the product AB∈Km×pAB\in K^{m\times p}AB∈Km×p to be its matrix. The defining action and entry formula are (AB)x=A(Bx),(AB)ij=∑k=1naikbkj.\begin{equation} \label{eq:19:product} (AB)x=A(Bx),\qquad (AB)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}b_{kj}. \end{equation}(AB)x=A(Bx),(AB)ij​=k=1∑n​aik​bkj​.(19.2) To derive the entries, apply the composition to ej∈Kpe_j\in K^pej​∈Kp. The column BejBe_jBej​ is the jjjth column of BBB. Its image under AAA has iiith entry ∑kaikbkj\sum_k a_{ik}b_{kj}∑k​aik​bkj​, which is the required entry of the jjjth product column. Thus the inner sizes must agree. The outer sizes give the product’s size. This definition extends the local 2×22\times22×2 product of Lecture 15.

For a rectangular example, take A=(1−12031),B=(201−1−12).A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&2\\0&3&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.A=(10​−13​21​),B=​21−1​0−12​​. The product ABABAB is 2×22\times22×2. Taking each row against each column gives AB=(−152−1).AB=\begin{pmatrix}-1&5\\2&-1\end{pmatrix}.AB=(−12​5−1​). In the other order, the product is 3×33\times33×3: BA=(2−241−41−170).BA=\begin{pmatrix}2&-2&4\\1&-4&1\\-1&7&0\end{pmatrix}.BA=​21−1​−2−47​410​​. For example, its middle row is the first row of AAA minus the second, namely (1,−1,2)−(0,3,1)=(1,−4,1)(1,-1,2)-(0,3,1)=(1,-4,1)(1,−1,2)−(0,3,1)=(1,−4,1).

Product laws

The identity matrix In∈Kn×nI_n\in K^{n\times n}In​∈Kn×n has entries 1 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere. Its action is Inx=xI_nx=xIn​x=x. Hence ImA=A=AInI_mA=A=AI_nIm​A=A=AIn​ for A∈Km×nA\in K^{m\times n}A∈Km×n, since both sides have the same action on every column. For compatible matrices, multiplication is associative: (AB)C=A(BC),A∈Km×n,B∈Kn×p,C∈Kp×q.\begin{equation} \label{eq:19:associative} (AB)C=A(BC),\qquad A\in K^{m\times n},\quad B\in K^{n\times p},\quad C\in K^{p\times q}. \end{equation}(AB)C=A(BC),A∈Km×n,B∈Kn×p,C∈Kp×q.(19.3) For x∈Kqx\in K^qx∈Kq, the left action is A(B(Cx))A(B(Cx))A(B(Cx)) by two applications of the product definition. The right action has exactly the same value. Equality of actions proves equality of the matrices.

The distributive laws and the scalar rule, whenever the indicated products and sums have matching sizes, are A(B+C)=AB+AC,(A+B)C=AC+BC,(cA)B=c(AB)=A(cB).A(B+C)=AB+AC,\qquad (A+B)C=AC+BC,\qquad (cA)B=c(AB)=A(cB).A(B+C)=AB+AC,(A+B)C=AC+BC,(cA)B=c(AB)=A(cB). For the first law, linearity of AAA gives A((B+C)x)=A(Bx+Cx)=A(Bx)+A(Cx)A((B+C)x)=A(Bx+Cx)=A(Bx)+A(Cx)A((B+C)x)=A(Bx+Cx)=A(Bx)+A(Cx). For the second, the entrywise sum acts by (A+B)(Cx)=A(Cx)+B(Cx)(A+B)(Cx)=A(Cx)+B(Cx)(A+B)(Cx)=A(Cx)+B(Cx). For the scalar rule, scalar multiplication of an action and linearity give (cA)(Bx)=cA(Bx)=A(cBx)(cA)(Bx)=cA(Bx)=A(cBx)(cA)(Bx)=cA(Bx)=A(cBx). Each proves the corresponding equality on all inputs, and therefore on matrices.

For a square matrix A∈Kn×nA\in K^{n\times n}A∈Kn×n, define its powers by A0=InA^0=I_nA0=In​ and Ar+1=ArAA^{r+1}=A^rAAr+1=ArA for integers r≥0r\geq0r≥0. Associativity gives ArAs=Ar+sA^rA^s=A^{r+s}ArAs=Ar+s for r,s≥0r,s\geq0r,s≥0: induction on sss starts with InI_nIn​, and multiplying the induction equality by AAA gives the next case. These powers are defined only for square matrices.

19.3 Order of multiplication and plane motions

Associativity does not permit changing the order of factors. For θ∈R\theta\in\Rθ∈R, the counterclockwise plane rotation has matrix Rθ=(cos⁡θ−sin⁡θsin⁡θcos⁡θ).R_\theta=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.Rθ​=(cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​). Indeed, its columns are the rotated standard unit columns, by the angle addition formulas of Standing Fact E. Those same formulas in the four product entries give RθRϕ=Rθ+ϕR_\theta R_\phi=R_{\theta+\phi}Rθ​Rϕ​=Rθ+ϕ​ for θ,ϕ∈R\theta,\phi\in\Rθ,ϕ∈R. For example, the upper-left entry is cos⁡θcos⁡ϕ−sin⁡θsin⁡ϕ=cos⁡(θ+ϕ)\cos\theta\cos\phi-\sin\theta\sin\phi=\cos(\theta+\phi)cosθcosϕ−sinθsinϕ=cos(θ+ϕ); the upper-right is −sin⁡(θ+ϕ)-\sin(\theta+\phi)−sin(θ+ϕ), the lower-left is sin⁡(θ+ϕ)\sin(\theta+\phi)sin(θ+ϕ), and the lower-right is cos⁡(θ+ϕ)\cos(\theta+\phi)cos(θ+ϕ).

The horizontal shear SSS sends (x,y)(x,y)(x,y) to (x+y,y)(x+y,y)(x+y,y). Its matrix and a quarter-turn matrix are S=(1101),R=Rπ/2=(0−110).S=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad R=R_{\pi/2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.S=(10​11​),R=Rπ/2​=(01​−10​). Figure 19.1 compares their successive actions on e1e_1e1​.

The products are RS=(0−111),SR=(1−110).RS=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad SR=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.RS=(01​−11​),SR=(11​−10​). Their first columns already differ. Therefore RS≠SRRS\ne SRRS=SR, although both orders have the same size.

A zero product also need not have a zero factor. For example, the nonzero matrices E=(1000),F=(0001)E=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad F=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}E=(10​00​),F=(00​01​) have EF=02×2EF=0_{2\times2}EF=02×2​. The action of FFF keeps only the second coordinate, which EEE sends to zero. Thus a product can vanish because the first action’s outputs all lie in the null space of the following action.

19.4 Transpose, adjoint and trace

For A∈Km×nA\in K^{m\times n}A∈Km×n, its transpose AT∈Kn×mA^{\mathsf T}\in K^{n\times m}AT∈Kn×m is given by (AT)ji=aij(A^{\mathsf T})_{ji}=a_{ij}(AT)ji​=aij​. For a complex matrix, its entrywise conjugate A‾\overline AA has entries aij‾\overline{a_{ij}}aij​​. Its adjoint is the conjugate transpose A†=A‾T,(A†)ji=aij‾.A^\dagger=\overline A^{\mathsf T},\qquad (A^\dagger)_{ji}=\overline{a_{ij}}.A†=AT,(A†)ji​=aij​​. For real matrices the adjoint equals the transpose. For example, C=(1+i2−i3−2i),C†=(1−ii23+2i).C=\begin{pmatrix}1+\ii&2\\-\ii&3-2\ii\end{pmatrix},\qquad C^\dagger=\begin{pmatrix}1-\ii&\ii\\2&3+2\ii\end{pmatrix}.C=(1+i−i​23−2i​),C†=(1−i2​i3+2i​). The two operations of conjugation and transposition have different roles: conjugation changes entries, while transposition interchanges their positions.

For A∈Km×nA\in K^{m\times n}A∈Km×n and B∈Kn×pB\in K^{n\times p}B∈Kn×p, the product rules are (AB)T=BTAT,(AB)†=B†A†.\begin{equation} \label{eq:19:reverse} (AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T},\qquad (AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger. \end{equation}(AB)T=BTAT,(AB)†=B†A†.(19.4) Both right sides have size p×mp\times mp×m. For the transpose, their entry in row jjj, column iii is ((AB)T)ji=∑k=1naikbkj=∑k=1n(BT)jk(AT)ki.\bigl((AB)^{\mathsf T}\bigr)_{ji} =\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}b_{kj} =\sum_{k=1}^n (B^{\mathsf T})_{jk}(A^{\mathsf T})_{ki}.((AB)T)ji​=k=1∑n​aik​bkj​=k=1∑n​(BT)jk​(AT)ki​. For the adjoint, conjugate the same finite sum. Conjugation preserves addition and products by Lecture 15, giving ∑k=1naikbkj‾=∑k=1nbkj‾ aik‾=(B†A†)ji.\overline{\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}b_{kj}} =\sum_{k=1}^n\overline{b_{kj}}\,\overline{a_{ik}} =(B^\dagger A^\dagger)_{ji}.k=1∑n​aik​bkj​​=k=1∑n​bkj​​aik​​=(B†A†)ji​. These entry identities prove both product rules. The definitions also give (AT)T=A(A^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}=A(AT)T=A and (A†)†=A(A^\dagger)^\dagger=A(A†)†=A, since each entry returns to its original position and conjugation twice returns the original scalar. For same-size matrices, transposition preserves sums and scalar multiples; the adjoint preserves sums but sends cAcAcA to c‾A†\overline c A^\daggercA†. Each assertion follows by applying its entry definition to the sum or multiple.

For a square matrix D∈Kn×nD\in K^{n\times n}D∈Kn×n, its trace is the diagonal sum tr⁡D=∑i=1ndii.\operatorname{tr}D=\sum_{i=1}^n d_{ii}.trD=i=1∑n​dii​. The trace of a rectangular matrix is not defined here. For A∈Km×nA\in K^{m\times n}A∈Km×n and B∈Kn×mB\in K^{n\times m}B∈Kn×m, both products are square, and the finite double sum proves tr⁡(AB)=∑i=1m∑j=1naijbji=∑j=1n∑i=1mbjiaij=tr⁡(BA).\begin{equation} \label{eq:19:trace} \operatorname{tr}(AB) =\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}b_{ji} =\sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{i=1}^m b_{ji}a_{ij} =\operatorname{tr}(BA). \end{equation}tr(AB)=i=1∑m​j=1∑n​aij​bji​=j=1∑n​i=1∑m​bji​aij​=tr(BA).(19.5) The middle equality interchanges finite sums and commutes scalar factors. It does not commute the matrices. In the rectangular example above, the traces are −1−1=−2-1-1=-2−1−1=−2 and 2−4+0=−22-4+0=-22−4+0=−2, even though the products have different sizes.

19.5 Elementary matrices and inverses

An elementary matrix of size mmm is obtained by performing one of Lecture 17’s elementary row operations on ImI_mIm​. If this operation exchanges rows iii and jjj, the resulting matrix has rows ejTe_j^{\mathsf T}ejT​ and eiTe_i^{\mathsf T}eiT​ in those positions. Its product with any A∈Km×nA\in K^{m\times n}A∈Km×n therefore exchanges those rows of AAA. If the operation multiplies row iii by c≠0c\ne0c=0, the corresponding row of the elementary matrix is ceiTce_i^{\mathsf T}ceiT​; its product row is ccc times row iii of AAA. If the operation adds ccc times row jjj to a different row iii, its row iii is eiT+cejTe_i^{\mathsf T}+ce_j^{\mathsf T}eiT​+cejT​; its product row is row iii of AAA plus ccc times row jjj. All other rows equal the corresponding coordinate rows and leave AAA unchanged. This proves that each elementary row operation is left multiplication by its elementary matrix.

For the earlier 2×32\times32×3 matrix AAA, adding three times row 1 to row 2 uses the elementary matrix TTT. The matrix and its resulting product are T=(1031),TA=(1−12307).T=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\3&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad TA=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&2\\3&0&7\end{pmatrix}.T=(13​01​),TA=(13​−10​27​). The new second row is 3(1,−1,2)+(0,3,1)=(3,0,7)3(1,-1,2)+(0,3,1)=(3,0,7)3(1,−1,2)+(0,3,1)=(3,0,7). For a sequence of operations with elementary matrices E1,…,ErE_1,\ldots,E_rE1​,…,Er​, the final array is Er⋯E1AE_r\cdots E_1AEr​⋯E1​A. The first operation is nearest to AAA because it acts first. The same statement applies to the whole augmented array [A∣b][A\mid b][A∣b].

A square matrix A∈Kn×nA\in K^{n\times n}A∈Kn×n is invertible if there exists a square matrix CCC satisfying both identities AC=CA=In.\begin{equation} \label{eq:19:inverse} AC=CA=I_n. \end{equation}AC=CA=In​.(19.6) Such a matrix is its inverse, denoted by A−1A^{-1}A−1. The inverse is unique. If CCC and DDD both satisfy the two identities, associativity gives C=C(AD)=(CA)D=DC=C(AD)=(CA)D=DC=C(AD)=(CA)D=D. Each elementary matrix has an inverse: take the elementary matrix of its reverse row operation from Lecture 17. Performing the operation and then its reverse returns every matrix, in particular ImI_mIm​, to itself. Reversing that order also does so, proving both products equal ImI_mIm​. For the displayed row-addition matrix TTT, the reverse operation subtracts three times row 1 from row 2.

For rotations the previously proved product formula gives RθR−θ=R−θRθ=R0=I2R_\theta R_{-\theta}=R_{-\theta}R_\theta=R_0=I_2Rθ​R−θ​=R−θ​Rθ​=R0​=I2​, so Rθ−1=R−θR_\theta^{-1}=R_{-\theta}Rθ−1​=R−θ​. Lecture 20 gives a general procedure to decide invertibility by elimination.

Summary

  • Equation (19.1): AxAxAx combines the columns of AAA with coefficients from xxx. A linear map’s unique standard matrix has columns T(ej)T(e_j)T(ej​).
  • Equations (19.2) and (19.3): compatible matrix multiplication represents composition and is associative. Factor order cannot generally be changed.
  • Equations (19.4) and (19.5): transpose and adjoint reverse product order. The trace identity holds for A∈Km×nA\in K^{m\times n}A∈Km×n and B∈Kn×mB\in K^{n\times m}B∈Kn×m.
  • Elementary matrices perform row operations by left multiplication. A sequence of operations has product Er⋯E1E_r\cdots E_1Er​⋯E1​ in the order of action.
  • Equation (19.6): an inverse satisfies both identities and is unique. Elementary matrices are invertible. The rotation inverse is R−θR_{-\theta}R−θ​.

Sheet 19 practices products, powers, traces and elementary operations. Lecture 20 uses these operations to construct an inverse.
